# Epilogue System
A Homebrew To Surpass Storyteller

# Character Details

## Depth

Depth can be thought of as your character's power level. It functions as a cap on your Attributes; you cannot increase your Attributes past your current Depth. Depth can be improved by growing as a character, usually through some sort of dramatic shift in their worldview or by bettering one's understanding of oneself. You need to achieve a Goal, first (see Progression).

Your GM will tell you what your character's Depth should be at the start of the campaign. The recommended default is 5 for reasonably skilled main characters who have been doing their thing for a while already.

## Attributes
Attributes are numbers that can be used in rolls.

Attributes are rated from one to ten (assuming baseline human capabilities), with 1 being minimal effectiveness and 10 being peak performance. If you have not put any points into an Attribute, it starts at 0. It costs 5 XP to go from zero to one, then 3 XP after that to keep raising that Attribute.

At character creation, you get a certain number of Attribute Points that can be spent 1-to-1 to gain dots in Attributes. 25 is a good default amount of Attribute Points to start with.

### Stats
Your character has a set of six Stats:
- Beefy: How big, strong, and physically fit you are.
- Speedy: How fast, agile, and good at getting around you are.
- Clever: How book-smart and witty you are.
- Trashy: How streetwise, snarky, and underhanded you are.
- Smooth: How much animal magnetism you have.
- Square: How good you are at being prim, proper, and professional.

You can come up with new Stats if you need to, but these six cover a wide enough spread that they should be enough for most use-cases.

### Skills
Skills are defined by a noun-verb--you can think of them like job titles. A Skill is a Thing Do-er. Some examples include:
- Driver: Operating ground vehicles.
- Shooter: Using a gun to get shit done.
- Hacker: Using computers in ways they weren't meant to be used.
- Talker: Being good with words.
- Understander: Being good with *people*.
- Doctor: Being good with people's insides.
- Executive: Because 'con artist' is impolite.

You can make up new Skills with GM approval. (Not every GM appreciates the idea of adding an "Intergalactic Warrior" to their campaign.)

### Sources
Sources represent things that are outside of you--stats are your innate tendencies, skills are your training, sources are your...well, resources. 

A Source can be a literal object or objects (like Money), a place you have control over (like a Nightclub), or a connection you can call on (like an Informant). They can also be abstract things that come and go, like Willpower.

#### Negative Sources
Sometimes a Source represents something you *don't* want. These are called Negative Sources. You can still use them in rolls where the GM allows it. They cannot be Burned like normal--instead, Burning a Negative Source on a roll *adds* another dot to the Negative Source, capping at ten.

A good way of marking a Source as negative is to put a dash in front of it, like this:
> -Exhaustion

#### Wounds
Wounds are a special kind of Negative Source. You suffer -1 dice on all rolls per Wound you currently have. When you have three or more Wounds and run out of HP again, you must make a roll of your choice with your Wounds as a dice penalty. If you succeed, add a Wound and continue as normal. If you fail this roll, you are rendered unconscious--and depending on what took you out, you might be bleeding to death. The GM is expected to use their discretion about what kinds of damage are lethal.

Wounds are removed one at a time. The amount of time and medical attention it takes to remove one Wound depends on how many Wounds you currently have. Consult the following table:

| Wounds             | Time                              |
|--------------------|-----------------------------------|
| 1 Wound            | First aid and a good night's rest |
| 2 Wounds           | First aid and 3 days of bed rest  |
| 3 Wounds           | A week in the hospital            |
| More than 3 Wounds | +1 week per additional wound      |

Supernatural healing or super-science can make this go faster. The GM may also decide to adjust the healing time or ignore it completely at their discretion, i.e., if the tone of the campaign makes extended hospital stays more annoying than dramatic, or if they think it should take more than a week to recover from having a hole in your chest.

If you have a Wound, you can Burn it like a Source, Skill, or Stat (see Burning under Rolling Dice). However, Wounds don't go away when Burned--each time you Burn a Wound, you gain a new Wound instead, just like with other Negative Sources. Make it count.

#### Temporary Sources
The GM may tell you to take a Temporary Source--this acts like a Source, but goes away when there's a scene transition (or when the GM tells you it goes away--for example, "take 3 dots of Stability as a temporary source until you move from your sniper nest").

Temporary Sources represent momentary advantages, whether in combat or out of it. Burning a Temporary Source is much less costly than burning other things (see Rolling Dice). They are also *NOT* capped by your Depth. This means that, early on, you can gain a lot more dice through Temporary Sources than you'd otherwise have access to.

Temporary Sources can also be Negative. Temporary Negative Sources behave just like regular Negative Sources, aside from eventually wearing off.

A good way of marking a Temporary Source is to put an exclamation mark on it.

## Hit Points
Your character starts with maximum HP equal to 5 plus their Depth.

If you run out of HP, you gain one Wound and refill your HP. Any leftover damage rolls over into the new 'HP bar'--if you have 10 HP and take 11 damage, you take a Wound and are left at 9 HP.

Armor will grant you Armor Points, or AP. AP is depleted before HP. AP recovers to full between scenes, but does *not* recover when you get Wounded.

## Hats
Hats provide additional special rules for what your character can do, usually with some kind of drawback or risk. Think of it as an extra role that your character can step into; a character wearing the Vampire Hat is also a vampire, a character wearing the Warrior Hat is also a Warrior, and a character wearing the Adventurer's Guild Hat is also in the adventurer's guild. A Hat consists of the following parts:

### Requirements
What you need to wear the Hat. Usually this includes a Skill at higher than 1 dot. It may also ask you to have "received" a Source or Skill--this means that you only need to have had that Source or Skill above 1 once, and you don't lose the Hat if you lose your last dot in that Source or Skill. Some Hats may have Stat requirements, too, and Hats may also require you to have experienced some key event or done something specific (such as signing up for a guild, being bitten by a vampire, or not doing harm to the innocent).

### Powers
What the Hat lets you do. Ideally this all fits onto a single page, but Hats might get complicated enough to require a whole rulebook of their own.

### Progression
This section describes how you unlock new Powers and usually includes a space for tracking how close you are to a new Power.

### Risks
This section summarizes the kinds of additional threats you take on by wearing this Hat. For example, a Vampire might have to deal with Vampire Politics, a Cyberpunk might get attacked by cops on sight, and so on. The more powerful the Hat, the worse the dangers. If you're lucky, the biggest danger will be your own hubris. Some Hats have easily-managed downsides, but there's always *something* here that can complicate your life.

# Character Creation

## The Short Version

1. Get your starting Depth and Stat Points from your GM.
2. Figure out what Hats you want for your character (and whether your GM will let you use them).
3. Allocate your Stat Points across your Attributes. Remember to spread your points across multiple Attributes, this is important for rolling dice.
4. Write down your HP, and make note of the Hats you're using. If a Hat requires additional setup, take care of that now.

## Starting Depth and Stat Points

Your starting Depth defines the scope of your character's power, as well as how 'deep' they are in the world's events--the kinds of connections they have, the kinds of foes they've fought, and the amount of influence they might wield. The suggested default Depth for new player characters is 5--not at the peak of performance yet, but reasonably capable.

Your stat points, on the other hand, define how 'broad' your skillset can get before your character becomes a jack of many trades and master of none. **These are not experience points**; they are spent 1 to 1 on Stats, Skills, and Sources alike. The suggested default is 25 stat points--at Depth 5 with 25 stat points, you can specialize hard into five Attributes, or pick 1 or 2 specialized stats and spread the rest of their points out across various options. More stat points make your character more versatile, fewer will force you to specialize more.

## Picking Hats

You should pick Hats you want to use ***BEFORE*** allocating stat points, since you'll often have requirements for those modules! Remember that GM approval is required for all Hats, as well.

## Allocating Points

As mentioned above, dots in Stats, Skills, and Sources all cost one stat point each. It's more cost-effective to get Stats or Skills, since Sources are cheaper to get with XP, but you should consider taking dots in all three categories.

## Final Touches

Your HP is equal to your Depth plus 5.

Write down all the Hats that your character has attached to them. Hats often come with special rules, so it's important to keep track of which ones are attached to your character. Some of them also require you to do additional setup, so take care of that now as well.

Don't forget to name your character.

# Rolling Dice

Dice are rolled to:
1. Resolve conflicts between characters.
2. Find out what happens if you do something risky.
3. Find out if you can perform under pressure.

In other words, if there's no stakes, don't roll dice. The average person doesn't need to make a Driver roll to get to work; they roll Driver when someone cuts across 3 lanes of highway traffic in front of them, or shoots out one of their tires, or straight up tries to run them off the road.

## Basic Rolls
A roll is described by two Attributes. You must pick Attributes from different groups; the roll can be a Stat and a Skill, a Stat and a Source, or a Skill and a Source. For instance, you might roll Smooth Driver to show off your sick drifting skills, or roll Clever Money to see if you can dodge consequences for financial crimes, or roll Money Talker to make yourself sound rich.

The higher of the two Attributes is how many dice you roll; the lower of the two Attributes is the Target Number. After the roll, each die that shows a value equal to or less than the Target Number is counted as one Success.

### An Example
Dawnathan J. Breeze has stolen a motorcycle from a local gang of biker ninjas, and is currently being pursued by their deadliest shinobi. He wants to do a sick jump off an empty car-carrier in front of him to create more space between himself and the ninjas…

Dawnathan: "So, I'm going to drive up the back of this--you know, that thing that they use to transport cars? This one's empty and it's basically a ramp on wheels. Then I'm gonna do a jump off the top. Would Smooth against Driver work for that?"

GM: "If you do some kinda trick while you're doing it, yeah. Otherwise you'd need Speedy against Driver."

Dawnathan: "What if I throw my arms up and throw the horns with both hands while I'm in the air?"

GM: "That works."

Dawnathan: "Okay, I'm rolling my Smooth of 3 and my Driver of 5, so that's five dice at TN 3..." [rolls dice] "Seven, five, four, four...three. One success."

GM: "We take those. You sail through the air and just barely land on an overpass. It's a rough landing, though, and the ninjas quickly figure out where you've gone. Take two Distance as a temp source."

### What To Do If You Don't Have Two Attributes

You *should* be able to construct usable rolls out of any two Attributes, but there may be situations where your character needs to do something that they don't have the training for. For example, your entirely mundane human might need to roll Magic against something to interfere with a ritual, or your cluelessly-archaic vampire might need to roll Hacker against something to read their email.

When you're trying to do something with an Attribute you don't actually have, that Attribute is treated as if you had a rating of 1, ***and*** you must subtract 1 from the total number of successes you get. If you're trying to do something with *TWO* Attributes you don't have...you'll need bonus dice from somewhere.

## Burning
Sometimes you absolutely have to push beyond your limits, because the short-term consequences outweigh the long-term costs. You may declare that you are *Burning* something when you are declaring your roll. The thing you burn has to be relevant to the action in some way. For example, you might burn Willpower to push through pain on a roll of Clever against Shooter when you've already taken a bullet, or you might burn a temporary source like Distance to leverage it into better positioning. You wouldn't be able to burn Hacker to get a better shot...unless you can come up with a really good reason.

When you Burn something as part of a roll, you add dice equal to the dots in the thing you Burned, then remove a dot from the thing you Burned. You can only Burn one dot per roll, and it must be a different Attribute than the ones you're using in the roll.

Burning dice *also* always increases the stakes of the roll in some way. If you were doing nonlethal damage before, your damage becomes lethal. If you were adding temporary Sources, they become permanent Sources. If you piss someone off? Well, now they REALLY want you gone...

### Burning Wounds and Negative Sources
Wounds and other Negative Sources can also be burnt, and have the same effects as burning anything else, with one caveat: when you burn a Wound or Negative Source, you *gain* a dot instead of losing a dot.

Note that as long as you have hit points, you're still in the fight, even if you have three or more Wounds...which means that the only downside of burning Wounds past your third is the stacking dice penalty.

### An Example
Dawnathan's gained some Distance, but he can't afford to rest on his laurels. However, he gets a risky idea...

Dawnathan: "I'm going to try and lose them by stopping in an alley. I'll be rolling Clever Driver, and I want to burn the Distance I just got."

GM: "Okay. Bear in mind that if you mess this up, you're still gonna lose that Distance, and the ninjas might be able to drag you into close combat as a result."

Dawnathan: "Yeah, but if this works, I'm golden. So, I have 3 Clever and 5 Driver--and because I burned that point of Distance, I add two dice, so that comes out to 7 dice at TN3." [rolls dice] "Two nines, two fives..2, 2, 1, three successes."

GM: "Awesome. You drift around a corner, then keep drifting--bringing yourself in line with an alley just past the corner. You roll into it and cut your engine, quickly hopping off the bike and walking it behind a dumpster. Moments later, you hear the biker ninjas screaming past. You're in the clear, for now."

## Dice Bonuses and Penalties
Another way conditions in a scene can affect your rolls is by adding or removing dice from your dice pool. Having the high ground in a swordfight might give you +1 dice on your roll of Speedy against Swordfighter, whereas gunfighting in a pitch-black room might give you -3 dice on your roll of Smooth against Shooter.

All bonuses and penalties are applied *after* the multiplier from Burning something. (Look on the bright side--this makes Burning something a great way to deal with a penalty that might otherwise cripple you.)

### Temporary Sources as Bonuses or Penalties
Many ongoing effects can be represented as Temporary Sources, positive or negative. These can then apply as bonuses or penalties on your rolls!

You can use an Action to place Temporary Sources on one or more characters; to do this, declare your roll as usual, as well as a name for the Temporary Source you're creating and who you're creating it for. You get to place two dots of that Temporary Source per success on your roll. You may divide these dots as you wish, within reason.

The GM is responsible for deciding which Temp Sources apply to any given roll. The only hard rule is that you cannot use a Temporary Source on a roll that's supposed to generate or remove that same Temporary Source--so, for instance, you can't make your opponent take 3 dots of Off Balance and then roll at a -3 from being Off Balance when they're trying to remove Off Balance. (This is especially important for Source Combat, where adding and removing Sources is the entire point!)

### Fighting Defensively & Reactions
Defending against incoming attacks does not use your regular action; instead, you have one *Reaction* per round, specifically for defensive options. There is no set roll for defending--you can use any stat, skill, or source you like, as long as the GM approves it--but it's generally accepted that Beefy or Speedy plus a combat Skill works in most Direct Combat situations.

#### Blocking
Your first defensive option is to Block. Each success on a Block roll applies a -1 dice penalty to incoming attacks until your next turn, as long as the roll makes sense for that attack. If you roll Clever against Hacker to Block an enemy hacker's attack on your mainframe, you can't use that result to *also* Block the wrench his buddy is trying to beat you with.

Blocking uses up your Reaction for the round, and can't be done more than once per round. This means that if you commit to defending against a certain kind of attack, smart opponents can outmaneuver you. This *also* applies to your attacks against an enemy who's defending.

#### Redirecting
Your other defensive option--riskier, but more valuable and flexible--is to Redirect. In order to successfully Redirect an incoming attack, you must meet or exceed the number of successes your attacker rolled--all-or-nothing. Either you redirected the attack or you didn't. Furthermore, Redirect only works against one attack at a time.

*However*, if you successfully Redirect an attack, *you get your Reaction back*, meaning you can keep Redirecting attacks until you fail--or Redirect one attack and then Block the next.

#### Improvising
Technically a defensive Action, but one with potential beyond merely defending, Improvising allows you to place Negative Sources in response to an enemy's action. Doing this consumes your Reaction just like Blocking does.

Improvising can *only* affect a single target, so it's less effective against groups, and unlike normal Temporary Source rolls, you only get to place one dot of Temporary Source per success on the roll. However, these Temporary Source dots behave just like they normally do, meaning that Improvised defenses can last multiple turns.

## Combo Actions
Another form of pushing past your limits, Combo Actions let you take dice penalties in exchange for getting to act multiple times in the same round. When you take a Combo Action, you take a dice penalty equal to the number of combo actions you've taken this turn, counting this one--so, your first Combo Action is at -1 dice, your second Combo Action is at -2 dice, and so on.

There are certain limitations on Combo Actions--weapons marked as Single Fire can only be used once per combat round, regardless of how many Combo Actions you can take, and the GM can veto actions that would physically be impossible (such as punching someone, then sprinting 100 feet to punch his friend before said friend can shoot you).

Most importantly, if a Combo Action roll fails, you can't take any more Combo Actions this round. (Don't worry; if your first action in a round fails, that doesn't lock you out of Combo Actions.)

Combo Actions can be incredibly powerful if used well, though it's best to have a large dicepool to work with. In particular, Combo Actions let you make attacks while defending.

### An Example

Later, Dawnathan corners one of the Biker Ninjas to interrogate him about the location of a valuable gemstone said to have been stolen by the leader of these motorhead shinobi. However, the ninja isn't going down without a fight…

GM: "The ninja's hand whips out to the side, loosing three kunai in your direction!"

Dawnathan: "I'm going to take my first action this round to dodge, using Speedy against Shooter."

GM: "Roll it."

Dawnathan: "5 Speedy against 6 Shooter." *rolls* "Three successes."

GM: "That's a -3 dice penalty on the ninja's attack. He only has 4 dice to work with--" [rolls] "But he gets a success on his one die, so you take two base damage plus 2 added damage, for a total of 4 damage from a kunai nicking your cheek."

Dawnathan: "After reaching up to wipe the blood away, I take a Combo Action to quickdraw my revolver and put a bullet through his kneecap. That's...Speedy against Shooter, and I take a -1 dice penalty because it's a Combo Action, right?"

GM: "Because it's your first Combo Action, yes. Roll it."

Dawnathan: "Four dice, then." *rolls* "Two successes! My revolver does 5 base damage plus two per success, so that comes out to 9."

GM: "The ninja howls in pain and clutches his ruined knee, but a true ninja never surrenders..."

## Heroic Actions
There's one last trick available to you when things are looking *really* dire. If you are about to be knocked out, you may declare that you are taking a Heroic Action. Then, choose an Attribute to wager. Make a roll with your wager against your Wounds, *ignoring all dice bonuses and penalties*.

**Whether the roll succeeds or fails**: You perform the heroic feat successfully, as if you had rolled twenty successes (the maximum possible in this system).

**If this roll is successful**: You clear all Wounds, recover all HP, and immediately take a combat turn. (This means you get to take your regular action after doing a Heroic Action.)

**If this roll fails**: The Attribute you wagered is set to zero, as if you'd burned all of your dots at once. Furthermore, the exertion renders you unable to continue, as if you were knocked unconscious. This happens even if you weren't in combat--you pushed yourself so far beyond your limits that it simply leaves you unable to continue in this scene.

# Combat and Other Forms of Conflict

## Types of Combat
Unless you're playing a one-player game set in the depths of space, your character will likely be in conflict with other characters. This comes in many forms, and isn't strictly limited to physical violence--conflict may come in the form of a rival businessman trying to financially outmaneuver you, a clash between a computer hacker and a defense program, or a trading of insults at a dinner party. For the sake of simplicity, these are divided into two categories:

### Direct Combat
This is anything that involves literal physical harm--punching, shooting, slashing, hurling fireballs, the works. 

In a Direct Combat, you can use weapons, and attacks are directed at a target's HP and Armor Points, meaning you can take Wounds from this form of combat.

GMs are advised to encourage creative actions by giving out Temporary Sources to represent tactical advantages or disadvantages--giving you dots of Cover to represent finding a good piece of cover, or dots of Position to represent gaining ground on an opponent in a chase, or dots of Smokescreen if you're hiding in a smoke cloud, and so on. Players should feel free to Burn or Stake these Temporary Sources when they don't want to risk their permanent Attributes, and are encouraged to use their combat turn for setting themselves or their allies up for future turns as well as just taking swings at the enemy.

#### Doing Damage
When you attack, you turn the number of successes on your attack roll into damage to your target's HP or AP.

By default, you do 1 damage per Success, as long as you have at least 1 Success (after applying Block and Redirect, of course).

Remember that Damage 'rolls over' when you deplete a target's HP and apply a Wound to them.

### Source Combat
This is anything that involves social maneuvering, attacking things you own rather than you, or other stuff that the average person might not realize is violence.

In a Source Combat, you're not rolling for damage--and weapons and armor are useless. Instead, you're rolling to do one of the following:
- Create a Temporary Source for yourself (two dots per success)
- Create a Temporary Negative Source for someone else (two dots per success)
- Move Temporary Sources around--i.e., stealing positive ones from your enemies or moving negative ones away from yourself (one dot moved per success)
- Consume 10 dots of Temporary Sources to create a permanent Source for yourself (making one dot per success)
- Consume 10 dots of Temporary Negative Sources on a target to create a permanent Negative Source on them (making one dot per success)

For example, you might hack a mainframe by rolling Smart against Hacker to generate Control (a Temporary Source), then using 10 dots of Control and a Smart against Hacker roll to generate several dots of Server Farm (a *permanent* Source, representing that you've secured a backdoor into that mainframe). Alternatively, you might roll Smooth against Talker to generate Frustration in someone you're insulting from across the dinner table, in order to trade them in for a Faux Pas--getting your target so pissed off that they say something they really shouldn't.

## Turn Order
Combat starts with whoever decides to strike first. Organize the characters in the combat into teams. Turn order alternates between teams; teammates can decide amongst themselves which character should go next. Try to spread turns evenly between teams, but each character gets one turn per combat round (with however many Combo Actions they can manage) and one Reaction per combat round.

# Progression
## Experience Costs
It costs 3 XP per dot of a Stat or Skill, 2 if it's a Source. It's that simple. If you had zero dots in the Attribute, you have to spend 5 XP to get the first dot back (assuming it's possible--you can't make the blind see for 5 XP).

## Experience Gain

### Per Session
If you're running formal sessions, you can gain up to 3 XP per session:
1. You gain 1 XP just for showing up.
2. You gain 1 XP if your character did something especially cool, dramatic, or befitting the campaign's theme.
3. You gain 1 XP if your character did something that set your character back, but made sense for them. This could be falling into a vice, making an honest mistake, or standing up for what your character believes in when it comes with a cost.

Smart players will notice that this means you can burn 1 Attribute dot per session, and as long as you do something interesting and something flawed during that session, you've lost nothing--or gained an XP, if you burned a Source. They'll also notice that if you lose all dots in an Attribute (say, by using Second Wind and failing the roll), you'll take at least two sessions to get your first dot back.

Smart *GMs* should try to get players participating as much as possible. The XP rewards for good roleplay are there to be incentives for the sort of player who prioritizes the crunchy parts of the game, but it's also the responsibility of the GM to encourage that good roleplay by playing along with it!

These rules assume that there will be 1-3 major scenes per session--enough for everyone to get a chance to do something interesting with their character, but not much more than that. Feel free to adjust XP gains to account for how much story you're going through per-session.

### Play-By-Post
If you're running a play-by-post game, or there's not really an "end of session" for some other reason, you can gain up to 1 XP per scene for either doing something cool/dramatic (like in point 2 above) or taking a setback for character reasons (as in point 3).

Scenes end by the agreement of the players in the scene. If in doubt about whether a scene has ended, use this as a rule of thumb: If all of the characters could have gone to the bathroom offscreen before the next thing happens, there's been a scene transition. Remember that you're playing a game, and you're ostensibly playing it with friends, so don't be a jerk about it.

### If there isn't a GM
If you don't have a GM, players may each grant one other player in the scene a point of XP for good roleplay; you're encouraged to tell your fellow players what you liked about how they played the scene out.

## Gaining Depth

Depth is special. You can't *just* buy it with XP. To gain Depth, you must set out and then complete a Goal.

### Goals
Goals are defined, personal things that your character wants to achieve. They need to be achievable (i.e., "I want to win a boxing match against my rival"), difficult (it's no fun if you already know you can curbstomp your rival!), and important to your character (none of that makes sense for someone who doesn't care about boxing). Work with your GM on this. It should also be something that fits in a sentence, though it's okay if the details are complicated.

Once you and the GM agree on a Goal, you also need to pay a 5 XP fee. This is mainly to keep you from achieving multiple Goals per session, because this is *supposed* to be how your character grows in the long term. The GM may waive this fee, or let you go into XP debt for it, at their discretion.

### Completing a Goal
This part is a bit of a misnomer, because there are several ways to 'complete' a Goal:

#### Achieving the Goal
Obviously, you can do what you set out to do. The GM has final say on whether you actually achieved your Goal, but they should be fair about it; if you beat your rival in a boxing match, you beat your rival in a boxing match.

#### Revising the Goal
Sometimes, what your character wanted to achieve turns out to be less important than they thought. Maybe, partway through training to beat your Rival, they fall gravely ill--or maybe you realize that they weren't the one who crippled your mentor years ago!

When this happens, you can ask the GM for permission to *revise* your Goal, which counts as completing it and lets you immediately create a new Goal.

#### Failing the Goal...?
Finally, sometimes you learn more by failing than by winning. You make it to the big fight, thinking you've finally got this in the bag...only for your rival to pull out a new technique that you weren't prepared for!

When a suitably dramatic failure happens, or failure makes the Goal no longer achievable (i.e., your rival retires after this fight), the GM may rule that you have gained Depth anyway because of the lessons you've learned from confronting that failure.

# Items and Item Rolls

Attributes can represent access to more concrete objects via a dice roll.

## Simple Items
If the item is nothing particularly unique--say, a common tool, regular clothes, et cetera--it's a Simple Item.

Simple Items can be produced any time they're needed by a roll that has the appropriate Attributes in it. Simple Items can also be produced as part of another roll when it makes sense (i.e., "I take out a set of lockpicks and get to work on the lock" would be a single roll, maybe Clever against Burglar, to both have lockpicks and pick the lock.)

If the roll fails, you can still get the item--but there's a catch of some kind, usually due to the item being cheaply manufactured or inconvenient to get to. Furthermore, if you absolutely *HAVE* to make sure you get the item exactly as you want it, you can burn a dot of a *Source* to get it without a roll--this represents a "money is no object" approach, spending a premium or putting extra pressure on your contacts.

More specific combinations make 'simple' stretch further--a roll of Money against Hacker would let you have high-end computer hardware at hand when that would normally be a Complex Item, whereas Money against Smooth would only get you standard consumer hardware.

If the item is especially cheap or easy to find, the GM may give you a dice bonus on this roll or even let you automatically succeed. (No sane GM would make you roll to have clothes on your back, unless you're just that broke...)

Stats for some Simple Item weapons and armor is included in the appendix.

## Complex Items
Things that are rarer or more difficult to find--high-end equipment for something you're not a specialist in, materials that only occur in certain parts of the world, and so on--are Complex Items. Like Simple Items, you set up a roll that fits the need being filled, but if the roll fails, you can't secure the item *at all*...under your normal budget. If you've failed a roll to get a Complex Item, you can still choose to burn a Source dot to get it anyway, just like with Simple Items. Once again, this represents blowing a *lot* of resources on getting this thing--Money gets tight, Connections become strained, and you'll have to work to get back to where you were.

Like with Simple Items, more specific combinations of Attributes make your rolls reach further. That Money-against-Hacker roll from the Simple Items example would *also* allow you to source the kind of cutting-edge computer hardware that would make the CIA blush.

Complex Items typically act like a Simple Item, but twice as effective as normal. 

## Unique Items
Items that are genuinely one-of-a-kind...have to be made to order, and that makes them Unique Items. You cannot roll to purchase a Unique Item; instead, you pay for its effects by burning one *or more* dots of Sources. Furthermore, you may take on some form of quest to pay for part of the Unique Item's effects, though you must still burn at least one dot of Sources.

The effects of Unique Items are determined at time of creation. You don't have to literally be the one creating the Unique Item--you use the same process when finding a rare artifact through your Contacts as you do when buying the materials for a new invention with Money. These are the sort of thing that players take on as long-term goals.

### Attributes On Unique Items
The simplest form of Unique Item is one that has its own Attributes. You can use the Unique Item's Attributes in your rolls, Burn them to gain bonus dice, and so on, much like any Attribute on your character sheet. However, increasing the Attributes of a Unique Item requires going through the same process you went through to get it.

The cost of adding Attributes to a Unique Item is one dot of payment for every 2 dots of attributes on the item.

### Special Effects On Unique Items
This is trickier. You'll have to work with the GM to get an idea of how powerful the effect is, and how much it should cost. Some guidelines:
- A dot of Temporary Source, or one bonus die, added *without* taking up your action, should cost one dot of Source.
- *Spending an action* to gain Temporary Source or bonus dice should net you two bonus dice/Temp Source per one dot of Source.
- Doing something reliably or without risk should only be possible if you get specific with it (i.e., "Open Any Door" is too much, "Open Electronic Locks" is acceptable). This should also cost at least two dots of Sources--more if the effect is especially powerful.
- Each Quest you take on to make the item should give you a one-dot discount, down to a minimum of one.

## Weapons, Armor, and Other Equipment
Some stuff has combat applications, obviously. Broadly, most things that can be used for combat can be divided into Weapons and Armor.

### Weapons
Some weapons may say that they do something "On Hit". This means that they do something *when they successfully do damage to a target*. On Hit effects apply once per attack, per target hit.

While you can only use one Weapon on each attack action, the amount of weapons you can *carry* is subject to GM discretion, since the exact details will vary depending on what kinds of weapons you have and the tone of the campaign. Everyone knows action movie heroes never run out of pocket space, but you might not be an action movie hero...

Weapons can have a number of other keywords attached to them as well. Some of these include:
- Spread X -- You can attack up to X targets with one roll. Effects that reduce dice on this roll, such as enemy Block reactions, affect the whole roll--meaning that one guy Blocking will reduce the effect of the roll against *everyone* you target.
- Steady X -- If you hit, this weapon does X damage, even if you roll fewer than X successes.
- Precise X -- Each success does X damage to the target instead of 1 damage.
- Shatter -- This weapon removes twice as much AP as it normally would.
- Meatgrinder -- This weapon removes twice as much HP as it normally would.
- Single Fire -- This weapon may only be used once per turn regardless of how many Combo Actions you take.
- Ranged -- This weapon can hit things that aren't within your reach.
- Reloading X -- After taking X attacks with this weapon without reloading, you have to reload it with a dice roll.
- Prepared -- You can take an action to Prepare this weapon. Roll as you would for an attack; if the roll succeeds, successes get added to your next attack roll's successes. If the roll fails, you lose all Prepare bonuses. You can keep doing this indefinitely, so long as you keep succeeding on the roll...

### Armor
Armor grants Armor Points, which act as an extra 'health bar' that doesn't give you a Wound when it runs out, but also doesn't refresh when you're Wounded.

Unless otherwise stated, you can only benefit from one piece of Armor at a time. If you somehow have multiple pieces of Armor in effect, add their AP together to get your AP total.

Some Armor may have keywords, much like Weapons do. Some of these keywords include:
- Patch X -- You can take an action to repair this armor in the middle of a fight. Make an appropriate roll, and if you get at least X successes, refresh your Armor Points.
- Recharge X -- Each turn, if you didn't take any damage, you add X Armor Points to your total, up to your armor's normal maximum.
- Resistance to [thing] -- Attacks from weapons or characters in the stated category deal half damage to you, even if your Armor Points are depleted.
- Vulnerable to [thing] -- Attacks from weapons or characters in the stated category deal double damage to you!

### Other Equipment
The vast majority of things that aren't Weapons or Armor can be represented by rolling to apply Temporary Sources.

If you *absolutely have to*, though: You may have one piece of Other Equipment in effect at a time, and it may have any keywords that Weapons or Armor can have. Anything on the Other Equipment applies as if it is on the Weapon or Armor you're using for an action--so if you have a piece of Other Equipment with Steady 2, all of your attacks behave as though your weapon has Steady 2.
