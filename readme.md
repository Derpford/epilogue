# Epilogue

A game to surpass Storyteller. Check [main.md](https://github.com/Derpford/epilogue/blob/main/main.md) for the main rules and the [hats](https://github.com/Derpford/epilogue/tree/main/hats) directory for Hats your character can wear.

# Quickstart Rules

1. Your character has three kinds of Attributes: Stats (adjectives, what you are), Skills (job titles, what you do), and Sources (nouns, things you have). The standard start has them at Depth 5. HP is equal to 5+Depth.
2. The standard start gives you 25 dots to invest in Attributes. You cannot have an Attribute higher than your Depth.
3. You can optionally add Hats to your character--these give you access to special rules in exchange for various risks and downsides. You need to fulfill the requirements and check with the GM to have your character wear a Hat; the GM is always allowed to say that a given Hat is inappropriate for this campaign (no, you can't be a computer hacker in the Wild West, unless your GM decides that cowboys with computers is cool).
4. To do something, describe what you're doing, then pick two Attributes from different categories--a Stat and a Skill, a Skill and a Source, a Source and a Stat, and so on. The higher of the two is how many dice you roll and the lower of the two is the success rating; each die that shows equal to or under the success rating is one success.
5. You can optionally Burn an Attribute to get that many dice added to your roll, at the cost of reducing that Attribute by 1 after you're done rolling (or *gaining* one, if it's a Negative Source). You can.
6. You can create Temporary Sources with your roll if you want to set up a future action for success, or create Temporary Negative Sources to make your enemies less able to do stuff. You get 2 dots of Temp Source for every 1 success on a roll, unless otherwise stated. Temporary Sources can be used like any other Attribute, for both rolling and Burning. Ten dots of a Temporary Source can also be converted into a Permanent Source with an appropriate diceroll.
7. When you are attacked, you can use your Reaction in one of three ways: you can Block (make a roll; until the start of your turn next round, any attack the roll would logically work against suffers a penalty equal to your block roll's successes); you can Redirect (make a roll; if you meet or beat the attacker's successes, you pick a new target for the attack and refresh your Reaction); or you can Improvise (make a roll to add 1 dot of Temp Source or Temp Negative Source per 1 success). You get one Reaction per combat round, though Redirect lets you get it back early.
8. If you get hit, the damage is subtracted from your HP. If your HP hits zero, reset it to full, add one dot of Wounds, then keep going with the leftover damage. (Yes, this means you can suffer multiple Wounds from one attack.) Wounds are a special Negative Source that can only be removed by getting medical treatment of some kind. If you run out of HP while having 3 or more Wounds, you must make a roll of your choice with a penalty to your dicepool equal to your current Wounds, and if you fail you're knocked out.
9. If all else fails, you can use a Heroic Action. Tell the GM what you want to do, then pick an Attribute and roll it with Wounds. You get what you want either way, but if the roll succeeds, you clear all Wounds and immediately take a free turn if you're in combat. If you fail, you are KO'd and you lose all dots in the Attribute you rolled with.

# Why?

I love the way that playing Mage: the Ascension feels, but its rulebook is utterly atrocious--contradicting itself in places, hiding essential info about playing the game deep inside the book, outright having *incorrect* rules (Matter 2 and 3 are swapped)--it's a testament to how cool the WoD games and setting are at heart that people put up with such frustrating rulebooks.

I *know* we can do better, so I set out to do it. My goals with Epilogue are as follows:
1. Keep it simple and flexible. This system should be good for any WoD game, as well as any campaign that wants to be narrative-first, dramatic, and full of gritty action-drama moments. It should also be easy to understand and reason about, while also being able to represent many different kinds of planning, prepwork, and interactions with the world.
2. Make big dicepools easier to access for newbies, and more controlled so that experienced players don't become balance nightmares. The biggest problem I had once I understood Mage's rules was that I didn't know about all the little quirks and tricks that let more experienced players break the spellcasting system over their knees.
3. Keep the lethality, but use a simpler damage model. I saw several players struggle *hard* with Storyteller's damage system, and while I really like the snowball-y nature of damage and the oneshot potential for how they shape how players think about combat, I want it to be easier to grasp for newbies. I shamelessly stole Lancer's HP system for this.
4. Have explicit support for multi-splat games and for hybrid characters. White Wolf appears to have been strongly against mixing splatbooks at the table, but in my (admittedly limited) experience with the game, mixed parties are where the *fun* is at. I took some inspiration from GURPS' flexible splatbooks when building the Hat system.
